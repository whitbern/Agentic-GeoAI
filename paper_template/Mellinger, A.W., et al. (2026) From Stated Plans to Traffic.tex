\section{Introduction}
\guide{Target about 900 words here. Build a narrowing argument from broad significance
down to a specific practical or scientific problem. Try to cite what prior work already shows, and
then point to an unresolved gap to justify why this specific analysis is needed. Support
claims with citations (as many as possible). The final two paragraphs have specific functions, e.g., (a) state
numbered research questions explicitly, and then (b) sketch the organization of the paper. Do not
put the roadmap before the RQs.}

Route planning and traffic management tend to rely on demand estimated from past behavior, particularly one's revealed preference. 
This can be suitable for a person's typical commute, but it is less useful for new routes, new locations, sudden events, or other route disruptions. 
Much of the prevailing research on traffic and origin-destination (OD) demand are built from using surveys [cite], GPS traces [cite], and other counts [cite]. 
This paper instead focuses on the potential of using stated preference data to predict traffic patterns, particularly in scenarios where traditional 
data sources may be limited or unavailable. Stated preference surveys can provide insights into how individuals might respond to hypothetical scenarios, 
such as changes in route availability or traffic conditions. This paper also examines whether using natural language processing techniques can improve the 
accuracy of these predictions. The literature in this field is nascent, but a few studies have begun to explore this potential when setting up simulations, 
such as (author's name's) paper using ChatSUMO and AgentSUMO [cite] or planning actions [cite], or travel-behavior inference [cite]. 
However, these have not established natural language as a standard OD source to directly create OD matrices froms stated intentions. 
The challenge lies in translating these stated intentions into accurate predictions of actual traffic behavior. For our purposes, we 
define stated intentions as the traveler's expressed plans, which may include specific routes, destinations, and times, while actual behavior 
refers to the observed traffic patterns that result from these intentions.  

Origin-destination flows can serve as a proxy for travel demands [Rong et al, 2024], but if what if a model knew those demands - in natural language - 
from the outset? Generally, traffic decision systems will not have indicators of a traveler's intent outside of knowing what location puts into a GPS
and checking against a gazetteer. Because of the difficulty in getting a vast volume of data either from social media or from surveys, we will create
a synthetic population and their stated intentions. (Survey methods are costly). We can use this synthetic population to use in a model that aims to capture the relationship 
between stated intentions. We will validate this against actual behavior, and then use a separate dataset to train and validate our proposed framework. 
Using a synthetic dataset allows us to control for various factors and test the robustness of our framework under different scenarios. 

The gap between stated intentions and actual behavior can be influenced by various factors, including individual decision-making processes, 
external conditions, and the inherent uncertainty in human behavior. One could use a traditional modeling or a machine learning appraoch 
to address these complexities, but many studies are now leaning toward using deep learning methods beacuse of their complex structure and 
is suitable for specially designed network structures like the one we creat in this model (Rong, et al). Because this is a complex issue, 
we aim to address the gap between statement intetions and behaviaor using an agentic GeoAI framework. We propose an agentic GeoAI 
framework that leverages the stated preference data so we can more accurately predict how individuals will respond to various traffic and route planning scenarios. 
By integrating natural language processing techniques with traditional traffic modeling approaches, we aim to bridge the gap between stated intentions and 
actual behavior. This approach not only enhances our understanding of traffic dynamics but also provides a foundation for developing more responsive and 
adaptive traffic management systems. An agentic-based framework can simulate individual decision-making processes, allowing for a more nuanced analysis of 
how different factors influence route choice and traffic flow. It can handle free-form language such as relative spatial references, vague location descriptions, 
implied or emphemeral temporal factors along with other natural language constructs that are difficult to encode in traditional models. 
In addition, using an agentic GeoAI framework allows for grounding the language in a spatial construct. It resolves ambiguities in natural 
language such as "my office downtown" against a traveler's profile and known locations in a place catalog. Then agents can snap these 
statements to zones and network nodes. While one could employ spatial analytic techniques to map these statements to a location, one would have to 
make assumptions about the traveler's profile and known locations to make rules for pattern matching and place-name lookups. This would be 
incredibly brittle if we were to have entities who use language such as "the usual place", "over near the cafe", and the like. An agentic framework allows 
for a more flexible and adaptive approach to grounding natural language in spatial contexts, enabling more accurate predictions of traffic behavior 
based on stated preferences. This grounding is essential for accurately interpreting stated preferences and translating them into actionable insights 
for traffic management and route planning. We also propose a whole framework over a single LLM call because we want to look up places in a catalog 
and query the traveler's network, computing travel times and distances rather than inventing coordinates or making assumptions about the traveler's profile. 
This is a more robust approach that can handle a wider range of natural language inputs and, in turn, provide more accurate predictions of 
potential routes and traffic. We can also implement checks and repairs such as testing whether a trip is temporally and spatially 
feasible and quickly flag and fix it rather an imputing an impossible trip into the OD matrix. In addition, an agentic framework can handle 
ambiguiity more explicitly than a single LLM call by asking for, if not requiring, clarification from the traveler. This is important because 
natural language can be inherently ambiguous, and one LLM call may not be able to resolve all ambiguities.


\textbf{Example Research Questions.} This paper addresses one primary research question and several follow-on questions, 
to include: (RQ1) To what extent can advance natural-language travel intentions be converted into spatially valid demand 
estimates that improve traffic management, compared with historical OD estimates, on typical and atypical days, using an agentic GeoAI workflow?
(RQ2) How accurately does the workflow resolve origins, destinations, and times, and place them in the correct hourly OD cells, 
and how much do agentic tools and checks improve on single-shot extraction? (RQ3) How do participation, adherence to stated plans, 
spatial coverage of participants, and day type affect the improvement in traffic outcomes? (RQ4) Where do the improvements in traffic outcomes fall, 
and do they concentrate in neighborhoods that are already well observed? (RQ5) And finally, do these findings hold when the network and 
place names are real rather than synthetic, and which grounding failures appear only with real geography?

\subsection{Synthetic population generation and traveler information}
A synthetic population of commuters are generated for this model with an emphasis on travel plans, and not the demographics of the people themselves. This
model focuses more on stated intentions of members of the population, not characteristics of the people. (Wang, et al, Embury et al,) While one's
characteristics would certainly influence the language a person uses, we will assume that any language with stated travel plans would have geographies 
that identify where something is in their localities. Each person is made of the following:
    - A home zone and work zone
    - Usual departure and return time
    - Schedule flexiblity, and how often they skip or change the trip
    - On an event day a scenario plan
These values are derived from the following places within each model: In the simulation model, there are 20 hand-set "types" for every 1000 people. "Type" 
refers to a shared home, work and schedule, not characteristics of people. In the network based on actual geographic locations, home-work pairs will be
derived from LODES. Departure times will be derived from ACS table B08302. Return time, flexiblity, and skip rate will remain as stated assumptions. This
construction of a synthetic population differs from other papers in that many start from PUMS and create an IPF or the like [Biramo and Torset, 2026 
(and others)]. They may reweigh PUMS to align with local ACS totals to produce profiles with age, income, vehicle types, and so on. THen an activity model
such as ActivitySim generates plans [cite]. This is unnecessary for this model because demographics do not change a traveler's lane decisions. When and where
people travel changes those decisions. In addition, PUMA is more coarse than what this model needs, which is decisions at the block level where LODES works best. 
If we wanted to assess how different groups use geographic language, then a PUMS analysis would be worth purusing but that is out of scope for this model. ssssssss
